# Research framework selector

Choose a framework by the decision it helps make. Confirm current versions and
field-specific extensions before a formal protocol or submission.

## Frame the question

| Question shape | Starting point | Use |
|---|---|---|
| Effect of an intervention | PICO: population, intervention, comparator, outcome | Systematic intervention reviews and answerable effect questions. See the [Cochrane Handbook, question framing](https://www.cochrane.org/authors/handbooks-and-manuals/handbook/current/chapter-02). |
| Association with an exposure | PECO: population, exposure, comparator, outcome | Exposure questions, including environmental and observational questions. See [NHMRC guidance](https://www.nhmrc.gov.au/guidelinesforguidelines/develop/forming-questions). |
| Map the extent or nature of a topic | PCC: population, concept, context | Scoping reviews. See the [JBI Manual for Evidence Synthesis](https://jbi-global.atlassian.net/wiki/spaces/MANUAL). |
| Other descriptive, predictive, mechanistic, qualitative, or design questions | No default mnemonic | Specify the phenomenon or population, setting and time, unit of analysis, outcome or construct, and intended inference. Add comparison only when it is meaningful. |

Do not use PICO as a universal research-question template. A review question
may need refinement as the evidence landscape becomes clearer; record material
changes and why they were made.

For primary empirical work, make the design logic explicit: target population or
system, unit of analysis, exposure or intervention, comparator (if relevant),
outcome, time horizon, and the descriptive, predictive, or causal quantity the
study is meant to estimate. For causal questions, a causal diagram can expose
assumptions about confounding and selection; for comparative intervention
effects estimated from observational data, consider the target-trial framework
where its assumptions and data requirements fit. These are design aids, not
automatic guarantees of identification. See [Causal Inference: What If](https://miguelhernan.org/whatifbook)
and the [target-trial framework](https://www.bmj.com/content/378/bmj-2022-071108).

## Choose an evidence-synthesis method

- For intervention-effect systematic reviews, use the [Cochrane Handbook](https://www.cochrane.org/handbook)
  for conduct and methods.
- For environmental management evidence syntheses, use the [CEE Guidelines](https://environmentalevidence.org/information-for-authors/).
- For scoping reviews, use the [JBI Manual](https://jbi-global.atlassian.net/wiki/spaces/MANUAL).
- For one-off orientation, narrative review, evidence mapping, realist review,
  or another synthesis, identify the purpose and method explicitly. Do not call
  it a systematic review unless its search, selection, appraisal, and synthesis
  methods support that label.

## Appraise evidence and certainty

Select risk-of-bias or critical-appraisal tools for the included study designs
and the specific result being used. Do not apply a trial checklist to
observational, qualitative, ecological, or modelling studies by default.
Separate limitations of an individual study from certainty across a body of
evidence. GRADE is a structured approach used widely in healthcare evidence
reviews and guidelines; use it where the field and question fit, not as a
universal score for every scientific corpus. See the [GRADE Working Group](https://www.gradeworkinggroup.org/).

## Select reporting guidance

First identify the study or synthesis design, then search the relevant
disciplinary reporting-guideline library, such as [EQUATOR](https://www.equator-network.org/).
Examples include:

- [PRISMA 2020](https://www.prisma-statement.org/prisma-2020) for reporting
  systematic reviews; use applicable extensions for the review type.
- [ROSES](https://www.roses-reporting.com/) for reporting systematic evidence
  syntheses in environmental research.
- Journal- and design-specific guidance for primary research.

A reporting checklist improves completeness of reporting. It does not prescribe
the full conduct of a study, establish methodological quality, or validate its
conclusions. PRISMA explicitly distinguishes reporting from conduct and
methodological appraisal; follow the relevant methods manual for those tasks.
